# Daniel Curran — Writing Style Guide (final)

One voice, two registers. This guide merges every known source of Daniel's writing:
the doctoral dissertation (*The Burial of the Dead*, 2020) and his professional
writing (Slack, Gmail, Google Docs, Slides, GitHub reviews). It replaces the older
separate guides and backs the qualitative rules with measurements taken from the
dissertation text itself.

## How to read this guide

- **Register 1 — Scholarly.** Long-form essays, criticism, and reflection. Drawn
  from the PhD and measured in the section "The scholarly register, measured".
- **Register 2 — Professional.** Workplace writing: Slack, email, proposals,
  memos, reviews, learner copy. Drawn from the professional samples.
- **Shared core.** What is true in both registers, whatever the task.
- Pick the register to match the task, then apply the shared core throughout.

---

## Shared core (both registers)

1. **Lead with the point.** In scholarship that is a topic sentence that makes a
   claim; at work it is the recommendation, decision, or what the reader needs to
   know. Never bury the conclusion.
2. **Concrete over abstract.** Evidence, tradeoffs, real examples, and named
   constraints beat slogans, grand claims, and vague transformation language.
3. **First person, naturally.** "I argue", "I suggest", "I'd like you to start",
   "we" for shared work. No passive voice, no performance of authority.
4. **Plain English around precise terms.** Use the exact domain word when the
   audience knows it; define any term the audience does not.
5. **Vary sentence length.** Long sentences carry the argument or the causal
   chain; short ones land the conclusion or the action.
6. **No em dashes.** This is the author's current explicit preference. Use a
   comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence instead.
7. **Semicolons are scarce; colons are not.** A colon introduces the elaboration,
   list, or consequence. A semicolon joins two tightly related clauses — use it
   sparingly.
8. **Bold only for titles and headings.** Never for emphasis inside a paragraph.
9. **Calibrate claims.** Hedge where the evidence hedges; do not overstate. Never
   invent facts or personal experience.
10. **The same skeleton in both registers.** Claim → evidence → analysis
    (scholarly) and point → reason → consequence → action (professional) are one
    shape wearing different clothes.

## Register 1 — Scholarly (the PhD)

### Voice and persona

Write in the first person: "I argue", "I suggest", "I contend", "I explore". Avoid
the passive voice. The "I" is present and active. Signpost the reader ("Over the
course of this essay", "In what follows", "I will now turn to", "As I discussed
earlier"). Hedge with conviction — "I suggest", "perhaps", "I contend that" —
balanced with confident claims, never over-qualified.

No contractions. Active voice. Formal register without stiffness: plain English,
not circumlocution.

### Sentence rhythm

Long, flowing sentences build the argument; short, punchy ones land conclusions.
The measured dissertation averages **28.6 words per sentence** (median 27), with
nearly half of all sentences in the 21–40 word range and a fifth above 40 words:

```
1–10 words   15%
11–20 words  18%
21–30 words  26%
31–40 words  21%
41–50 words  11%
51+ words     9%
```

Long sentences are the default, short ones the emphasis — not the reverse.

### Transitions (measured)

Open sentences with transitions freely, but follow the actual ranking, not a
generic list. The most common openers across the dissertation, in order:

**However (66), Yet (53), Furthermore (32), In this sense (28), Moreover (26),
Indeed (18), Instead (17), Rather (11).**

"Thus", "Consequently", "Additionally", and "Nevertheless" are rare (3–5 uses
each). "Therefore" is essentially absent. Lean on **However / Yet / Moreover /
Furthermore**, and use **In this sense / In other words** to rephrase a point.

### Hedging and conviction (measured)

"I argue" (14) and "I suggest" (12) are the signature verbs, with "perhaps" (25)
as the main qualifier. "I will" (47) carries the signposting ("I will now turn
to", "I will argue"). By contrast, **"I contend" appears once and "I propose" and
"maybe" never** — do not reach for them as if they were habits.

### Punctuation (measured)

Colons appear **about four times as often as semicolons** (396 vs 102, roughly
5 vs 1 per thousand words). Use the colon to introduce the explanation or
elaboration; use the semicolon rarely, only for two clauses that must stay bound.

### Rhetorical devices

- **Parallel constructions** for balance: "not only … but also" (38 uses), "both …
  and" (111 uses). "Neither … nor" is rare (7).
- **Evocative metaphor** is welcome, sparingly, and concrete: "the violence of the
  unspeakable", "the anonymity of oblivion", "the darkness of the death-chamber".
  It sharpens an abstract argument; it is not decoration.
- **Rhetorical questions** are rare — at most one per piece, for emphasis.

### Argument and structure

- Open each paragraph with a topic sentence that makes a claim, not an
  announcement.
- Build paragraphs as **claim → evidence → analysis → conclusion**.
- Use quotations as evidence, analysed immediately — never left to stand alone.
- Embed short quotes inline; block-quote only passages of four or more lines.
- Frame your contribution against existing work: "While critics have noted X, I
  argue Y."
- Define technical terms on first use, then use them.
- Write lists as prose ("ambiguity, indeterminacy and narratives riddled with
  self-contradiction"), never as bullets in the body.

### Citations

Parenthetical, lean, varied verbs: "According to X", "As X argues", "X writes
that". Cite the key idea, not every source in the chain.

### Blog adaptation (scholarly register, applied to the web)

When the scholarly register is aimed at a blog audience:

- **Titles** are quotations or provocative fragments, not descriptive labels
  ("I began at the beginning", not "Introduction").
- **Citations** are leaner still — the key idea, not every link in the chain.
- **Metaphor** leans in harder; the blog audience rewards vivid, concrete
  language more than the academy does.
- **Paragraphs** are tighter — three to six sentences, not the longer blocks of
  a dissertation.
- **Links** are inline hyperlinks, not footnotes.

## Register 2 — Professional (Slack, email, docs, reviews)

### Voice and persona

An approachable, technically literate colleague who thinks like a curriculum
designer and a pragmatic manager. Lead with the point, explain the learner or
business consequence, then specify what needs to happen. Assert direction without
performing authority: "I'd like you to start work…" is followed by the approved
scope, a document, and when to discuss it. In reviews, give a concrete reason a
change helps a reader rather than an unexplained verdict. Use "we" for shared
work.

### Tone by channel

- **Slack / working chat:** direct, informal, cooperative. Acknowledge the other
  person before giving the action. "Good stuff", "No worries", "Cheers", "Cool"
  are plausible, not mandatory.
- **Proposal / memo:** measured and constructive. State the recommendation, why
  now, the audience, scope, exclusions, and next steps. Acknowledge tradeoffs and
  uncertainty instead of overselling.
- **External email:** courteous but not florid. Thank the recipient, describe the
  decision boundary, then ask specific questions or name a next step.
- **Learner copy:** more encouraging and explanatory, with second-person "you"
  and a concrete outcome.

### Sentence structure

Conversational chat runs short — clipped units averaging around 8 words — while
polished documents run 18–28 words per prose sentence. Use a simple or compound
sentence for the claim and action; add one longer complex sentence for the causal
chain, condition, or constraint. Fragments are fine in notes, slide labels, and
fast chat, never as the default in polished prose.

### Vocabulary

Plain English around precise domain terms — skill badge, skill check, lab
sandbox, learning objective, stakeholder, Instruqt, Atlas, ASP. Define unfamiliar
abbreviations once. Prefer tangible verbs — **build, review, check, align,
confirm, replace, test, share** — over generic transformation language. Name the
audience, system, timing, and constraint where useful.

### Punctuation and formatting

- Headings for purpose-driven sections; bullets or numbered lists for objectives,
  sequences, questions, and next steps. Keep short Slack replies as short
  paragraphs, not mini-documents.
- Colons introduce a scoped list or label. Parentheses add a qualification.
  Questions are genuine requests, not decorative hooks. Semicolons are uncommon;
  exclamation points occasional and never in business analysis.
- **No em dashes.** Bold only for project titles and section headings.

### Rhetoric

Argue from **contrast and consequence**, not metaphor or hyperbole: current state
versus proposed state, guided recipe versus independent practice, activity versus
demonstrable skill. Analogies are occasional, not a signature. Do not manufacture
wit, poetic language, or grand claims.

## Reconciliation (how the registers differ)

| Dimension | Scholarly | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence length | ~29 words, flowing | 8–28, clipped |
| Metaphor | Evocative, welcome | Occasional, never ornamental |
| Hedging | "I argue", "I suggest", "perhaps" | "I'd like you to…", direct scope |
| Person | "I" | "I" + "we" for shared work |
| Formatting | Prose paragraphs, lists in prose | Headings + bullets |
| Length of claim | Claim → evidence → analysis | Point → reason → consequence → action |

These are the same writer at different distances from the reader. Do not let the
registers bleed: a memo should not read like a dissertation chapter, and an essay
should not read like a Jira ticket.

---

## Final-pass checklist

- Does the first sentence say what the reader needs to know (or make the claim)?
- Is the reason or consequence clear, with a specific next step?
- Is the tone right for the register — essay, Slack, email, proposal, review, or
  learner copy?
- Are claims calibrated and terms accurate for the audience?
- In scholarly prose: are quotations analysed, citations parenthetical, and
  transitions weighted toward However / Yet / Moreover / Furthermore?
- Any em dashes, ornamental metaphors, excessive exclamation points, or bolded
  emphasis to remove?
